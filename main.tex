\section{Idea 5: Sustainability-Aware OS Scheduling for Servers}

\subsection*{Introduction}
We propose extending operating-system schedulers on servers to optimize for
\emph{sustainability}, not just throughput or fairness. The core observation is that the cost
of running a job is not constant: electricity prices and the carbon intensity of the grid
vary throughout the day, and a server's available capacity changes as its utilization
fluctuates. A scheduler that is aware of these factors can shift work toward cheaper,
cleaner, less-congested time windows.

\subsection*{Core idea}
We divide server time into discrete \emph{blocks} (which may be fixed or dynamically sized).
Each block has an associated cost derived from:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Electricity price} at that time of day, which is non-uniform across the day.
\item \textbf{Carbon intensity} of the grid at that time (to minimize CO$_2$).
\item \textbf{Current utilization} of the server --- the remaining capacity below 100\%.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The result behaves like a \textbf{market}: when load is low, running a job is
cheaper; when the server is busy or electricity is expensive, it costs more. Users are
thereby incentivized to schedule flexible work into off-peak, low-carbon windows.

\subsection*{Scheduling guidelines}
\begin{enumerate}
\item No job is killed once it has been submitted and admitted.
\item Minimize CO$_2$ emissions across all scheduled work.
\item Price tracks load and time of day --- lower load means lower cost, like a stock market.
\item Encourage longer, batched jobs. We want users to consolidate work so it can run as a
group, improving efficiency.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{User submission}
A user submits a job request with the following parameters:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Preferred time of day} --- when the user would like the job to run.
\item \textbf{Estimated length} --- the job's expected duration, from the user's perspective.
\item \textbf{Accuracy score} --- a measure of how accurate this user's previous length
estimates have been (their track record).
\end{itemize}

\noindent The accuracy score feeds into a \textbf{penalty/reward (``punishment'')}
mechanism: users with a history of accurate estimates are treated more favorably, while
consistently inaccurate estimates incur a penalty. This discourages users from gaming the
system with misleading job lengths.

\subsection*{Scheduler inputs}
The scheduler computes its decisions from:
\begin{itemize}
\item Current \textbf{load} and \textbf{utilization} of the server.
\item \textbf{Cost of a block} and \textbf{length of a block}.
\item \textbf{Time of day}.
\item \textbf{Cost per user per task} (with the goal of nudging users toward longer, grouped
jobs).
\item User's historical \textbf{estimate accuracy}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Prior Work, Evidence, and Novelty}

This section checks each underlying premise of the idea against the literature
(web search) and pins down exactly what is novel.

\subsection*{Claims and evidence (the premises hold)}

\paragraph{C1 --- Electricity price is strongly non-uniform across the day.}
\emph{Supported.} Day-ahead wholesale prices follow a systematic intra-day shape:
midday prices collapse toward $\sim$\$15/MWh while the 5--8pm evening ``neck''
reaches $\sim$\$60/MWh on CAISO ($\sim$4$\times$ spread), and CAISO SP15 saw
$\sim$530 hours of negative prices in 2023 ($\sim$6\% of all hours)
\cite{r1,r2}. Demand and real-time prices swing at 5-minute granularity \cite{r3}.

\paragraph{C2 --- Grid carbon intensity varies a lot over the day and by region.}
\emph{Supported.} Hourly intensity spans $\sim$0 to $\sim$1{,}158 gCO$_2$/kWh across
$\sim$300 zones (2024) \cite{r4}. California's intra-day swing reaches
$\approx$19$\times$ min-to-max (solar midday dip vs.\ fossil evening ramp); China
$\approx$1.9$\times$ \cite{r5}. Time-shifting on hourly intensity cuts footprint in
all 65 grids studied, by 7\% (Mid-Atlantic) to 50\% (California) \cite{r6}.

\paragraph{C3 --- The workload to be scheduled is large and growing.}
\emph{Supported.} Data centres used $\sim$415 TWh ($\approx$1.5\% of global
electricity) in 2024, projected to $\sim$945 TWh ($\approx$3\%) by 2030; their share
of global CO$_2$ is $\sim$0.5\% today, heading to $\sim$1\% by 2030 \cite{r7,r8}.

\paragraph{C4 --- Servers run at low utilization and are not energy-proportional.}
\emph{Supported.} Servers spend most time at 10--50\% utilization, yet an idle server
still draws roughly half its peak power --- energy does not scale down with load
\cite{r9}. Low load is therefore both common and expensive, which is exactly the
regime a congestion price should target.

Each individual ingredient of our price already exists: price-aware placement
\cite{r20,r21}, a combined dollar-and-carbon objective \cite{r23}, market/auction
allocation \cite{r25,r26,r27}, and utilization/congestion pricing \cite{r28}.
Lov\'asz et al.\ \cite{r23} is the closest: it already optimizes price + carbon
jointly --- but across a network of datacenters, without a live-utilization
congestion term, a user reputation signal, or a no-kill guarantee.

\paragraph{C. User runtime estimates, accuracy, and reputation/penalty.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Backfilling needs user runtime estimates, and they are notoriously inaccurate.
EASY backfilling reserves slots using user estimates \cite{r29}; in the SP2 study
$\sim$19--20\% of jobs underestimated and would have been killed at the limit
\cite{r29}.
\item Estimates are crudely ``modal'': $\sim$20 distinct values cover $\sim$90\% of
jobs (round numbers + the queue max), so they barely distinguish jobs \cite{r30}.
\item System/ML predictions beat user estimates for backfilling --- EASY++ improves
performance up to $\sim$28\% \cite{r31}; ML predictors cut average bounded slowdown
$\sim$28\% \cite{r35}; but ML still underestimates 20--50\% of the time, needing
underestimation-aware loss \cite{r36}.
\item Most important for us: the canonical fix (Tsafrir et al.\ \cite{r31}) is to
decouple the scheduling prediction from the kill-time --- keep the user's generous
requested wallclock as the (rarely-hit) limit while scheduling on a shorter
prediction. This is essentially the mechanism our ``no-kill'' guarantee formalizes.
\item Reputation/penalty has direct precedent: ``Incentives to Tighten the Runtime
Estimates of EASY'' \cite{r34}. Sobering result: when the kill-threat was removed and
accuracy was rewarded, only about half of users improved and overall accuracy did not
rise much --- inaccuracy is partly inherent \cite{r32,r33}.
\end{itemize}
And yes --- mainstream schedulers \emph{do} kill over-limit jobs, which is the
practice our design rejects: Slurm terminates a job past its \texttt{--time} (state
TIMEOUT, SIGTERM then SIGKILL) \cite{r37}; PBS/Torque kills jobs exceeding walltime
\cite{r38}.

\paragraph{D. No-kill admission, deadlines, and batching.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Admit-and-commit (no-kill) is a solved theory problem. ``Efficient Online
Scheduling for Deadline-Sensitive Jobs'' \cite{r40} and follow-ups give online
mechanisms where once a job is admitted it is guaranteed to finish, never preempted
away \cite{r41,r42}.
\item Renewable-aware admission control that defers rather than kills already exists:
Cucumber \cite{r39} admits a delay-tolerant job only if it can finish by its deadline
on forecast renewable surplus, else queues it.
\item Production schedulers defer/requeue batch work (Google Borg evicts low-priority
batch but reschedules it \cite{r43}; Apollo coordinates $>$20K scheduling req/s
\cite{r44}) --- whereas spot/preemptible VMs \emph{do} kill: AWS Spot gives a 2-minute
notice \cite{r27}, GCP preemptibles are force-killed at 24h \cite{r46}.
\item Grouping/gang scheduling improves efficiency over many small jobs by cutting
fragmentation and per-job overhead \cite{r45}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{What we add (our contribution / novelty)}
None of the four ingredients is new alone; the contribution is the combination, the
layer, and the guarantees:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A single \textbf{OS-level block price} that fuses all three signals ---
electricity price \emph{and} carbon intensity \emph{and} live local utilization ---
as one market clearing price. Prior systems combine at most two (e.g.\ price+carbon
across datacenters \cite{r23}, or utilization-only congestion pricing \cite{r28}); the
utilization congestion term on a single server, justified by energy
non-proportionality (C4) \cite{r9}, is the under-explored piece.
\item A \textbf{per-user estimate-accuracy reputation folded into pricing/admission},
not just into runtime prediction. The HPC line predicts runtimes \cite{r31,r35} or
offers one-off incentives \cite{r34}; making a persistent reputation a first-class
input to the cost function is the new twist --- tempered by the evidence that
incentives move only $\sim$half of users \cite{r32,r33}.
\item A \textbf{hard no-kill guarantee as a design axiom}, realized by decoupling the
scheduling prediction from a generous kill-time \cite{r31} and by admit-and-commit
admission control \cite{r40,r39}.
\item Pushing the whole market down to the \textbf{OS/server scheduler} (per-block,
per-user, per-server), versus the cluster/datacenter-network granularity of
essentially all prior carbon/price schedulers \cite{r11,r23,r12}.
\item An \textbf{incentive to consolidate into longer, grouped jobs}, charged per
user-task, grounded in gang-scheduling efficiency \cite{r45}.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Framing for the defense:} novelty is the integration point (OS) + the triple
price (price $\times$ carbon $\times$ utilization) + reputation-in-the-price + no-kill
axiom --- not carbon-aware scheduling, market scheduling, or runtime prediction in
isolation, each of which is prior art above.

\subsection*{Caveats to state carefully}
\begin{itemize}
\item Closest prior art to acknowledge head-on: Lov\'asz et al.\ \cite{r23}
(price+carbon objective), Cucumber \cite{r39} (defer-not-kill admission), and the
EASY-incentives line \cite{r34}.
\item The reputation lever may be weak: controlled studies show incentives improved
only $\sim$half of users and barely moved aggregate accuracy \cite{r32,r33}; budget
for ML-assisted prediction \cite{r31,r35} as a fallback.
\item No-kill + deadlines + high load can be infeasible: the admit-and-commit theory
needs sufficient capacity / selective admission \cite{r40}; be explicit about the
admission policy that keeps the guarantee sound.
\item Signals are external and forecast-based: price and carbon come from markets /
WattTime / Electricity Maps and carry forecast error; the scheduler must be robust to
it.
\item Quantify against a real baseline: show CO$_2$/cost savings vs.\ FIFO/fair-share,
as the carbon/price papers do (7--50\% \cite{r6}, 33.7\% \cite{r12}, 51\% \cite{r13}).
\end{itemize}
